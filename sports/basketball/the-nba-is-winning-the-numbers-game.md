# The NBA Is Winning the Numbers Game—and Losing Basketball

*The abuse of the gather step, the disappearance of the midrange jumper and the epidemic of flopping have transformed the world's greatest basketball league. The players have never been more talented. So why does the game increasingly feel like something has been lost?*

**November 5, 2026 · Sports & culture · Opinion**

There was a time when basketball was a game of five players, two baskets and a relatively straightforward proposition: You had to dribble the ball to move it, beat your defender to score and absorb a certain amount of contact along the way.

Today, apparently, all three principles are negotiable. Watch an NBA game long enough and you will witness something extraordinary. A player will collect a dribble, cover a remarkable distance without bouncing the basketball, and glide toward the rim while the broadcast crew debates whether his journey constituted two steps, three steps or an entirely new interpretation of human locomotion.

On another possession, an open 16-foot jumper will be passed up in favor of a contested three-pointer. Moments later, a defender will collapse to the hardwood after contact that might not have disturbed a folding chair.

The game continues. The scoreboard lights up. The highlights circulate.

And somewhere along the way, basketball loses a little more of its identity.

This is not an argument that today’s players lack skill. Quite the opposite. The modern NBA features an extraordinary concentration of athleticism, shooting ability, positional versatility and basketball intelligence. Guards routinely make shots that previous generations would scarcely have attempted.

But extraordinary talent does not automatically produce extraordinary basketball.

The NBA has spent the better part of two decades optimizing its product for offensive efficiency, scoring and spectacular highlights. In the process, it has allowed three increasingly troubling developments to alter the character of the sport: the manipulation of fundamental movement rules, the marginalization of the midrange game and the normalization of theatrical contact.

Individually, each may appear manageable. Together, they represent something more consequential.

The NBA has become increasingly effective at producing points while becoming less disciplined about preserving the game that makes those points meaningful.

The Gather Step: When Traveling Became a Matter of Interpretation On October 28, 2025, Milwaukee’s Giannis Antetokounmpo drove toward the basket against the New York Knicks. What followed appeared, to many viewers, to be a remarkable sequence of steps without a dribble. NBC analysts Reggie Miller and Jamal Crawford watched the replay in disbelief. that much runway.

The play went viral. Fans counted steps. Former players questioned the officiating. Others defended the play by referencing the NBA’s gather-step rule.

And that last detail reveals the actual problem. Under the NBA’s official rules, a player moving with the ball may take two steps after gathering it. The gather occurs when a dribbler secures sufficient control of the ball, including placing two hands on it, allowing it to come to rest or putting a hand underneath it and bringing it to a pause.

A foot already on the floor at the moment of the gather does not count as the first of those two subsequent steps. Properly executed, this permits a legal and sophisticated collection of moves, including the Eurostep and certain step-back jumpers. The NBA formalized its gather definition in 2019, clarifying an interpretation officials had   Cost of Efficiency used for years.

The problem is not that every unconventional move is illegal. It is that the boundary between a live dribble and a completed gather has become extraordinarily difficult for ordinary spectators to recognize—and, at times, seemingly difficult for officials to enforce.

A player can manipulate the ball’s position, delay the appearance of full control and create additional separation before the two-step count even begins. Combine that with inconsistently enforced carrying and pivot-foot violations, and the defense faces a potentially devastating disadvantage.

The offensive player knows exactly when he intends to gather. The defender must anticipate a movement sequence whose legal starting point may be impossible to recognize in real time.

What was once a contest of footwork increasingly resembles a contest over the definition of possession. The issue reaches well beyond professional basketball. Young players learn the game by imitating their heroes. They see the step-backs, hesitations and extended drives, but often lack the technical understanding to distinguish a legal gather from an illegal travel.

That creates a peculiar educational contradiction. A youth coach teaches players to establish a pivot foot, execute a controlled jump stop and move without traveling. Then those same children watch professional highlights that appear to contradict the instruction.

The NBA cannot afford to dismiss that confusion as merely a failure of fans to understand complicated rules. A sport’s fundamental principles should not require forensic video analysis to remain credible.

Basketball has always rewarded creativity. But creativity is most meaningful when it operates within boundaries. Without those boundaries, skill and exploitation become increasingly difficult to distinguish.

The Disappearing Midrange Jumper—and the The midrange jump shot used to be one of basketball’s defining artistic expressions.

Michael Jordan’s turnaround. Kobe Bryant’s footwork. Dirk Nowitzki’s one-legged fadeaway. Rip Hamilton racing around screens before rising from the elbow.

Those plays represented something more substantial than two points.

They required timing, balance, spatial awareness, deception and an understanding of how to defeat a defender without necessarily overpowering him.

Today’s NBA has not eliminated those abilities. It has increasingly eliminated the incentives to demonstrate them. Consider the mathematics.

A player shooting 36% from three-point range generates 1.08 points per attempt. A player making 50% of his two-point jumpers generates only one point per attempt, before accounting for fouls and other possession effects. In a league governed by increasingly sophisticated analytics, the conclusion was predictable: Shoot more threes, attack the basket and avoid the comparatively inefficient territory between them.

The results have been dramatic.

In the 2000–01 season, NBA teams attempted approximately 13.7 three-pointers per game. By 2025–26, that figure had reached 37.0. More than 41% of all field-goal attempts came from beyond the arc, according to historical NBA game-log analysis.

A 2026 academic study of NBA shot locations documented the corresponding redistribution of attempts toward the rim and three-point line, with a substantial decline in shots from the intervening areas of the court.

The numbers demonstrate a profound strategic transformation.

But they do not establish that basketball has improved as a spectator experience.

The three-point revolution initially represented extraordinary innovation. Stephen Curry and the Golden State Warriors expanded the boundaries of what an offense could accomplish. Houston demonstrated how shot selection could become a competitive advantage.

Then everyone copied the formula.

The league’s offensive diversity began to narrow. Teams increasingly sought similar shots, occupied similar spaces and constructed similar offensive systems. The distinctions between organizations became harder to recognize through the movement on the court.

Even NBA Commissioner Adam Silver acknowledged the danger in January 2025, expressing concern that increasingly similar offenses could erode the individual identities that once distinguished teams.

He had identified the central contradiction of the analytics era.

What makes an individual team more efficient can make the entire league less interesting.

A player taking an open midrange jumper is not necessarily making a poor basketball decision simply because another location carries a higher average expected return. Context matters. Matchups matter. Defensive positioning matters. Late-game possessions matter.

And perhaps most importantly, unpredictability matters.

The midrange jumper forces defenders to respect the entire offensive floor. It rewards players capable of changing direction, rising into space and executing difficult shots against disciplined coverage.

Kevin Durant, DeMar DeRozan and Shai Gilgeous-Alexander demonstrate that this dimension of basketball remains both viable and beautiful.

Yet the league’s general philosophy increasingly treats those abilities as exceptions to an otherwise established formula.

That is not progress without cost.

It is specialization masquerading as completeness. A game dominated by layups, dunks and three-pointers may maximize mathematical efficiency while systematically reducing the variety of ways a player can demonstrate mastery.

Basketball should not have to choose between good mathematics and beautiful basketball.

The best version of the sport accommodates both. Flopping: When Deception Became an Offensive Skill There may be no better illustration of the NBA’s struggle with competitive integrity than the modern art of drawing a foul.

Contact has always been part of basketball. So has exaggeration. Players have attempted to influence referees for generations.

But today’s game has developed an increasingly elaborate vocabulary of manufactured reactions.

The sudden head snap. The dramatic backward fall. The airborne leg movement. The theatrical collapse following minimal contact.

Sometimes legitimate fouls accompany these reactions. Other times the performance itself is intended to create the impression of a foul.

The distinction matters.

Basketball is supposed to reward a player for defeating an opponent through skill, positioning or physical execution. Flopping rewards a player for persuading an official that something occurred which may not have occurred at all. It changes the nature of competition.

A defender making an appropriate contest can suddenly be punished because an offensive player successfully manufactures the appearance of excessive contact. Rather than playing the ball, defenders must also account for how their actions might be dramatized.

The incentives become perverse.

The league recognizes the problem. In 2023–24, it introduced an in-game flopping penalty and made it permanent in July 2024. An identified flop results in a non-unsportsmanlike technical foul and one free throw for the opposing team.

Yet enforcement remains a challenge.

For the 2026–27 season, the NBA renewed its emphasis on eliminating theatrical and exaggerated movements designed to influence officials. Its own basketball operations leadership acknowledged that flopping detracts from how the game is experienced.

Minnesota Timberwolves coach Chris Finch put the frustration more plainly before the season: Fans were tired of watching it, and officials needed to stop rewarding it. The NBA’s latest guidance distinguishes between permissible embellishment of actual contact and the more theatrical movements classified as flopping.

That distinction is understandable from an officiating perspective.

But it also exposes the problem.

The league has become so accustomed to players attempting to sell contact that it now formally differentiates acceptable salesmanship from unacceptable theatricality. Consider what that says about the culture of the sport. The issue is not whether every player who falls is pretending. Basketball is physical, unpredictable and

occasionally dangerous. Players must be permitted to protect themselves, and legitimate fouls deserve whistles. The issue is whether exaggeration should become an expected part of a player’s offensive toolkit.

No serious basketball coach would teach a young player that the foundation of good offense is convincing a referee to award undeserved free throws.

Yet the professional incentives can encourage precisely that behavior.

And when the world’s best basketball league rewards theatrical conduct, it becomes harder to explain why the same behavior should be discouraged everywhere else. The Business Is Thriving. That Doesn’t Mean the Basketball Is.

There is an important distinction between criticizing the NBA’s style of play and declaring that the league itself is failing.

Commercially, the evidence does not support a simple story of collapse.

The 2025–26 NBA regular season averaged approximately 1.78 million viewers across its primary national media partners, up 16% from the previous season, according to Sports Business Journal. The addition of NBC and Amazon expanded exposure, while the league reported strong attendance and digital engagement.

Basketball remains an enormously valuable global entertainment property.

Indeed, that success may be part of the problem. For the NBA, the incentives are straightforward: Deliver exciting highlights, cultivate international superstars, generate engagement and maintain an entertainment product attractive to broadcasters and sponsors. A spectacular dunk following an extended gather can generate millions of views, regardless of whether the movement raises questions about traveling.

A barrage of three-pointers creates explosive scoring runs and dramatic comebacks.

A controversial foul can dominate social-media conversation for days.

None of those outcomes is necessarily bad for business. But commercial success and competitive integrity are not interchangeable.

The NBA risks measuring the health of basketball through the popularity of basketball content rather than the quality of basketball itself.

There is also a generational dimension to this debate. Older fans sometimes romanticize the past, forgetting the slow possessions, excessive isolation and rough defensive play that made previous eras less appealing. Today’s players are better shooters, more versatile athletes and beneficiaries of improved training and strategy. A return to the 1990s would not automatically improve the modern game.

Nor should it be the objective.

The goal should be to preserve the principles that made basketball compelling in every era while allowing the sport to evolve.

That requires something more difficult than nostalgia. It requires reform.

Five Changes That Could Restore the Game The NBA does not need a wholesale reinvention. Its most urgent problems are largely matters of incentives, interpretation and enforcement.

First, restore credibility to traveling and carrying rules. The gather step should remain legal when properly executed. The Eurostep is a legitimate basketball skill. So is a well-executed step-back jumper.

But the league must enforce the actual moment of ball control, including pauses and carries, rather than allowing the gather to become an elastic concept. Pivot-foot violations should receive comparable attention.

Publicly distributed video examples, consistent officiating directives and transparent explanations of disputed plays

would help establish a standard understood by players and fans alike.

Second, protect the defender’s right to compete. The NBA should consistently enforce offensive push-offs, illegal screens and unnatural attempts to initiate contact. The league’s 2026–27 emphasis on these issues is a constructive step. But credibility will depend on whether enforcement survives the regular season and carries into the playoffs.

A defender who establishes legal position should not be placed at a systematic disadvantage simply because the league prefers offensive highlights.

Third, make deliberate flopping a losing proposition. The existing technical-foul system provides a foundation, but the NBA should strengthen consistent in-game enforcement and meaningful penalties for repeat offenders. Officials should resist awarding calls created primarily by unnatural offensive movements, without ignoring legitimate contact or compromising player safety.

If exaggeration no longer reliably produces an advantage, players will have less reason to practice it.

Fourth, rebuild appreciation for the complete offensive game.

The NBA cannot—and should not—mandate midrange jump shots. But it can encourage the development and recognition of complete offensive skills.

At youth, high-school and developmental levels, players should continue learning footwork, pivoting, post play, movement without the ball, controlled pull-up jumpers and the ability to score from multiple areas.

Analytics should inform shot selection, not become a philosophy that discourages entire categories of basketball skill.

Finally, evaluate rule changes through the quality of competition, not simply scoring.

Moving the three-point line, changing court geometry or adjusting defensive rules may eventually deserve serious experimentation.

But these changes should be tested carefully, including in developmental settings, before altering the NBA’s fundamental structure.

The league should measure not only offensive efficiency but also shot diversity, defensive competitiveness, stoppages, disputed movement violations and the consistency with which its rules are applied.

The objective should not be fewer points.

It should be better basketball.

The Game Is Worth Saving Because the Talent Is Worth Celebrating Perhaps the greatest irony of the modern NBA is that its problems are occurring during an era of astonishing individual ability.

Nikola Jokic demonstrates that a center can possess the vision and imagination of an elite point guard. Stephen Curry revolutionized the geometry of the court. Victor Wembanyama is redefining the physical possibilities of the sport.

These are not signs of basketball’s decline.

They are evidence of its remarkable potential.

But that potential deserves a competitive environment in which fundamental skills matter, defenders can challenge offensive players fairly and the outcome of a possession is determined more by execution than by the manipulation of rules or officials.

The purpose of preserving basketball’s purity is not to freeze the game in a previous decade.

It is to ensure that the sport’s evolution does not undermine the principles that made it great.

A beautiful midrange jumper should remain a legitimate weapon. A defender should be entitled to hold legal position. Two steps should mean two steps after a clearly established gather. And falling to the floor should not become a more reliable scoring strategy than making a basketball play.

The league has proved that it can manufacture more points, more highlights and more engagement than ever before.

What it has not yet proved is that those achievements necessarily make for better basketball.

That distinction should matter to the players, the owners, the commissioner and especially the generations of young athletes learning the game today.

Because basketball’s greatness has never depended solely on how many points are scored.

It has depended on how they are earned.

And if the NBA intends to preserve the integrity of the world’s greatest basketball competition, that is where its attention must return.

The future of basketball should belong to the players who master the game—not the players who master its loopholes.
